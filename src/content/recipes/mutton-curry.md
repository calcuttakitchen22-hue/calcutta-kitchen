description: "Bengali Sunday mutton curry with potatoes and well-koshano masala, pressure-cooked until tender and finished with garam masala, green chilli and ghee."
about:
  - "This Bengali Sunday mutton curry is built around the stages before the pressure cooker closes. The potatoes are fried first, sugar is lightly caramelised in mustard oil, onions are browned evenly, powdered spices go in as a wet slurry, and the mutton is koshano until the masala turns thick and clings to the meat with visible oil. Potatoes and green chillies return before approximately 350–400 ml hot water is added, then the curry pressure-cooks for tenderness. Because there is no reduction after pressure cooking, the gravy level must be judged before closing the lid. Bengali garam masala, fresh slit green chillies and ghee go in at the end, followed by a 10-minute covered rest off heat."

  - "Slice the onions to a similar thickness so they brown more evenly"
  - "Mix the powdered spices with a little water before adding them to reduce the chance of scorching and to distribute them more evenly"
  - "Do not judge koshano only by time; look for reduced moisture, thick masala clinging to the meat, deeper colour and visible oil"
  - "Use green chillies in stages so some of the fresher chilli aroma remains at the finish"
  - "Because there is no post-pressure reduction, judge the final gravy quantity before closing the cooker"
  - "Pressure-cooking time varies with the meat, cut and cooker, so avoid treating a whistle count as universal"
  - "Allow the pressure to release naturally before opening"
  - "After adding garam masala, fresh chilli and ghee, rest the curry covered off heat for about 10 minutes"
  - "This version does not use cumin powder"
faq:
  - question: "Why is sugar added to the mustard oil?"
    answer: "The sugar is lightly caramelised to deepen colour and add a subtle caramelised note. It is not there to make the curry sweet and it does not replace proper onion browning."
  - question: "Why are the onions sliced evenly?"
    answer: "Similar-thickness slices brown at a similar rate, reducing the chance of having burnt pieces mixed with undercooked ones."
  - question: "Why make a wet spice slurry?"
    answer: "Mixing the powdered spices with a little water lowers the chance of them scorching immediately on contact with the hot pan and helps them spread through the masala more evenly."
  - question: "How do I know the mutton has been koshano enough?"
    answer: "Look for the progression rather than only the clock: excess moisture reduces, the masala becomes thick and clings to the meat, the colour deepens and oil becomes visible."
  - question: "Can I use a fixed whistle count for pressure cooking?"
    answer: "No. This method uses about 18–22 minutes on low-medium heat after pressure is established, but the exact time varies with the meat, cut and cooker."
  - question: "Why are green chillies added twice?"
    answer: "The earlier chillies cook into the curry, while the fresh slit chillies at the end preserve more of their fresh aroma."
  - question: "Why must the water quantity be judged before pressure cooking?"
    answer: "There is no post-pressure reduction in this method, so the liquid level before the lid closes largely determines the final gravy quantity."
  - question: "Why use natural pressure release?"
    answer: "This recipe specifically allows the pressure to fall naturally before opening rather than forcing the cooker open."
  - question: "Why rest the curry after adding garam masala, chilli and ghee?"
    answer: "The final seasonings are added off heat, then the curry is covered for about 10 minutes before serving."
  - question: "Can I use smaller mutton pieces?"
    answer: "Yes. The larger pieces in this version are deliberately chosen for presentation; home cooks can use smaller curry-cut pieces."
